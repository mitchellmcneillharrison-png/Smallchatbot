The lighthouse stood at the edge of the cliff, its lamp turning slowly through the fog.
Every evening, old Mara climbed the spiral stairs to light it, counting each step out
of habit rather than need. She had climbed those stairs for thirty years, and her feet
knew the way better than her eyes did.

Her apprentice, a quiet boy named Teo, followed a few steps behind, carrying a lantern
of his own. He had come to the lighthouse in the spring, sent by his uncle to learn a
trade that still mattered. Mara did not talk much, but when she did, Teo listened
carefully, because she never wasted a word.

"The sea does not care if you are ready," Mara said one night, watching the waves
crash against the rocks below. "It only cares if the light is on."

Teo nodded, though he did not fully understand. He was still learning to read the
weather, to tell a passing squall from a storm that would test the old stone tower
to its foundations. Mara had promised to teach him everything she knew, but some
lessons, she said, could only be learned by standing watch through a long, dark night.

One evening, the wind rose without warning. Clouds rolled in from the west, thick and
low, swallowing the last of the daylight. Mara and Teo climbed the stairs together,
faster than usual, the lantern flames dancing wildly in the draft.

At the top, Mara checked the great lamp, trimming the wick with practiced hands.
"Tonight will be rough," she said. "Watch the horizon. If you see a light out there
that should not be moving, wake me at once."

Teo took his place by the window, wrapped in a wool coat against the cold. Rain began
to fall, first in scattered drops, then in sheets that rattled the glass. He thought
of the ships that might be out there, invisible in the storm, trusting a single
turning light to guide them home.

Hours passed. The wind screamed around the tower, and twice Teo thought he saw
something flicker on the water, only to lose it again in the dark. He kept the lamp
burning, kept his eyes on the horizon, and did not call for Mara, though his hands
shook with cold and worry.

Near midnight, a flare rose from far out at sea, a thin orange line against the black
sky. Teo's heart jumped. He ran to wake Mara, who was already sitting up, listening.

"A ship," she said simply, and together they lit the secondary signal lamp, the one
reserved for emergencies, and began the long process of tracking the flare's position
and preparing to send word to the harbor below.

By dawn, the storm had passed. A small fishing boat, its mast broken, limped into the
harbor under tow, its crew exhausted but alive. They said the lighthouse beam had been
the only steady thing in a night of chaos, a fixed point they steered toward when
everything else was churning water and wind.

Mara said nothing about it the next evening, only checked the lamp as always, counting
her steps up the spiral stairs. But she let Teo climb ahead of her that night, and
when they reached the top, she handed him the logbook and the pen.

"Write what happened," she said. "Someday you will be the one standing watch, and
whoever comes after you should know what the light is for."

Teo wrote carefully, describing the flare, the storm, the long hours of watching. When
he finished, Mara read it over and nodded once, satisfied.

"You did well," she said. "The sea will test you again. It always does. But you did
not look away, and that is the whole of the job."

They stood together at the window as the sun rose over the water, the lamp still
turning behind them, its light fading now against the brightening sky, ready to be
lit again when darkness returned.
